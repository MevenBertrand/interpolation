\begin{abstract}
Craig's Interpolation theorem has a wide range of applications, from mathematical logic to computer science. Proof-theoretical techniques for establishing interpolation usually follow a method first introduced by Maehara for the Sequent Calculus and then adapted by Prawitz to Natural Deduction. 

The result can be strengthened to a proof-relevant version, taking proof terms into account: this was first established by \v Cubri\' c in the simply-typed lambda-calculus with sums and more recently extended to linear, classical and intuitionistic sequent calculi.

In the present paper, we give a new proof of \v Cubri\' c's proof-relevant interpolation theorem by building on principles of bidirectional typing, and formalise
it in \Rocq.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subparagraph*{Craig-Lyndon Interpolation}

Craig's interpolation theorem~\cite{Craig57,Craig57a} expresses a desirable, though unsurprising, property of logical entailment: a logical entailment of $B$ from $A$ can be mediated through a logical statement that only uses the \emph{common concepts} of $A$ and $B$. More precisely, a \emph{Craig Interpolant} $I$ of $A \vdash B$ is a logical formula that is built using only the predicate symbols occurs both  in $A$ and $B$ and such that $A \vdash I$ and $I \vdash B$.

This was soon recognized as a central result of first-order logic~\cite{Feferman08,vanBenthem08} with application in both mathematical logic~\cite{Robinson56,Beth1953padoa,Feferman08,Vaananen08} (notably allowing an elegant proof of Beth' definability theorem and of Robinson joint consistency theorem) and computer science~\cite{Lavalette08,Troelstra_Schwichtenberg_2000,McMillan18} (with applications in database query planning or modularisation of model-checking) and numerous generalizations to extensions or restrictions of classical logic~\cite{Vaananen08,Schutte,Prawitz1965-PRANDA}.

Proof-theoretic methods for establishing Craig's theorem essentially relied on the admissibility of cuts in the sequent calculus and on the subformula property~\cite{Maehara,Prawitz1965-PRANDA}. This very fact, providing very well-structured methods~\cite{Maehara,Prawitz1965-PRANDA,Takeuti2013proof,Schutte,Troelstra_Schwichtenberg_2000}
certainly plays an important role in the ability to generalise the interpolation theorem to various logics as mentioned above, as well as in the in the occurrence of various finer-grained notions of interpolations, most notably (i) Lyndon interpolation~\cite{Lyndon1959interpolation}, a notion of interpolation that is sensitive to the polarity of occurrences of predicate symbols, and (ii) Uniform interpolation~\cite{PittsXXX}, a version of the interpolation property in which, given a formula $A$, an single interpolant can be generated, uniformly, for a whole sub-language.

\subparagraph*{Proof-relevant Interpolation for the \stlcp}
Considering the importance of proof-theoretic methods in the field of interpolation it is not so surprising that interpolation theorems can in some cases be given a proof-relevant refinement as recently advocated by Saurin~\cite{Saurin25fscd}. This means that not only can one factor the logical entailment through the interpolant formula, but also that the proof of $A\vdash B$ itself can be factored through two proof from and to the interpolant that, once composed, can be equated to the original proof. Even more interestingly maybe is the fact that the proof of such proof-relevant interpolation result was implicit in the original proof-theoretic results from the 60's by Maehara.

This is exactly what was at stake with \v Cubri\'c interpolation theorem~\cite{Cubricphd,Cubric1994} for the simply typed lambda-calculus with sums and empty types (\stlcp) that is a proof-relevant refinement of Prawitz' proof of interpolation~\cite{Prawitz1965-PRANDA} for natural deduction.\as{(Comment the semantical results by \v Cubri\'c as well!!!)}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Interpolation is cool
  \item It can be made proof-relevant, cf \cite{Cubric1994} for \stlcp
\end{itemize}

\subparagraph*{Interpolation meets normal forms}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The original proof in \Cubric~\cite{Cubricphd,Cubric1994} is… not very clean
  \item We can, however, discern that it follows the structure of
    normal forms
  \item By making this characterisation explicit and inductive, we obtain a much
    clearer proof
\end{itemize}

\subparagraph*{Normalisation for commuting conversion}

The key difficulty is that \(\beta\)-normal forms are not enough: interpolation
works well for terms/proofs which satisfy the subformula property, but in
\stlcp \(\beta\)-normal forms do not have this, we need to go further and
normalise with respect to \emph{commuting conversion}.

\subparagraph*{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We develop the meta-theory of \stlcp, in particular normalisation
    for a calculus with commuting conversion
  \item We use this as a basis for a new proof of proof-relevant interpolation
    for the \stlcp
  \item All our work is formalised in \Rocq
\end{itemize}

  \label{def:lang}
  A \emph{language} \(\lang\) consists of:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item a set of base types \(\base \in \Set\);
    \item a set \(\const \in \Set\) of constants;
    \item types for each constant, \ie

\begin{definition}[Types and terms]
\label{def:types-terms}
The definition of types and terms is given in \cref{fig:stlc}.

We work with parallel substitutions, the action of which we denote
\(\subs{t}{\sigma}\).
Although we use variable names for exposition,
we rather think of them in the de Bruijn style \cite{deBruijn1972}, and
formalise them this way in \Rocq. Accordingly, the
substitution calculus we use is essentially name-free, see \cref{fig:subst} for
its primitives. The typing judgment \(\typing{\Delta}{\sigma}[\Gamma]\)
means that \(\sigma\) take a variable \((x : A) \in \Gamma\) to a
term \(\sigma(x) \in \Tm(\Delta,A)\). The primitive's types should be already
informative, but let us describe them succinctly: \(\id\) is the identity,
mapping each variable to itself; \(\sigma \pcomp \tau\) is composition,
mapping \(x\) to \(\subs{\sigma(x)}{\tau}\); \(\wk\) drops the last
variable in context, and maps all other variables to themselves;%
\footnote{In named syntax this is a no-op, but we include it
as it changes the context in which a term is considered.} \(\sigma,t\) extends
a substitution \(\sigma\) by mapping the last variable in context to \(t\);
\(\Up \sigma\) maps the last variable to itself, and acts as \(\sigma\) otherwise;
finally \(t..\) maps the last variable to \(t\) and
acts as the identity otherwise. The following substitution rule is admissible:
\begin{mathpar}
  \inferdef[Subst]{\typing{\Delta}{\sigma}[\Gamma] \\ \typing{\Gamma}{\sigma}[A]}
    {\typing{\Delta}{\subs{t}{\sigma}}[A]}
\end{mathpar}

\emph{Renamings}, denoted \(\Ren(\Delta,\Gamma)\), are the substitutions that map
variables to variables.

\subsection{Reduction and equations}
\label{sec:red-eq}

The language defined in \cref{fig:stlc}, quotiented by the adequate equational
theory, is the internal language of bi-cartesian closed categories (biCCC), \ie
with finite products, finite coproducts, and exponentials. Normalisation
for this equational theory is unwieldy due to \(\eta\) laws for positive types
(see Scherer~\cite{Scherer2017}). Fortunately,
we can get away with normalisation for a less powerful reduction
containing only the rules known as \emph{commuting conversions} \cite{Girard1989}.
This is because normal forms for this reduction have a good enough subformula
property, even though they are not unique representatives for the equational
theory of biCCCs.

\paragraph*{Reduction}

To factor commuting conversions, we use \emph{eliminations},
given by the following grammar:
\[ e \Coloneqq \sapp{t} \mid \sproj{i} \mid \site{t}{t}\]
The \emph{zip} of a term against an elimination \(\plug{e}{t}\) is defined in the obvious way.

Reductions, then, are of two kinds: the usual \(\beta\)-reductions, corresponding
to computation ---applied abstraction, projection of a pair,
and branching on a constructor--- and commuting conversions, which push
an elimination across another one of a positive type (\ie \(\bot\) or
a sum). In the case of \(\bot\), the elimination gets completely removed, as it
is unnecessary: a proof of \(\bot\) can be used to inhabit
any type, there is no need to further eliminate it. In the case of the sum,
the elimination gets duplicated in each branch of the if.
\begin{definition}[Reductions]
  \emph{One-step reduction} \(\oredop\)
  is the congruent closure of the rules given in \cref{fig:stlc-red}.
  \emph{Reduction} \(\redop\) is the reflexive, transitive closure of one-step reduction.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Confluence]
Reduction is confluent: if \(\red{t}{u}\) and \(\red{t}{u'}\), then there exists
\(v\) such that \(\red{u}{v}\) and \(\red{u'}{v}\).
\end{theorem}

Since confluence is rather standard \cite{Girard1989}, yet tedious to prove, and
very orthogonal to our main focus, we do not formally prove it in \Rocq,
but only provide a pen-and-paper proof.
\begin{proof}
  We use the Hindley-Rosen lemma \cite[Prop. 3.3.5]{Barendregt1985}, by which
  it suffices to show that 1. \(\beta\)-reduction is confluent 2. commuting
  conversion is confluent 3. the two commute. The first is easy, since there is
  no critical pair, so the Takahashi variant of parallel reduction à la Tait--Martin-Löf
  \cite{Tait1967,Takahashi1995} does the trick. For the second, strong normalisation
  of commuting conversion can be shown by a simple \(\mathbb{N}\)-valued measure
  \mlb{formalisation}, so by Newman's lemma \cite[3.1.42]{Barendregt1985}, it
  suffices to show local confluence. This is painful due to the many critical
  pairs, so we have checked this with the automated tool \textsf{CSI\textsuperscript{ho}}
  \cite{Nagele2017}. Finally, commutation is again simple because
  critical pairs between \(\beta\) and commuting conversion can be closed in one step.
  They all are akin to the following, which is easily closed:

  \begin{tikzcd}[sep=small]
    & \ite{(\inl{a})}{b_l}{b_r}~u
      \arrow[squiggly,rd]
      \arrow[squiggly,ld] & \\
    \ite{(\inl{a})}{b_l~\subs{u}{\wk}}{b_r~\subs{u}{\wk}}
      \arrow[squiggly,rd]
      && \subs{b_l}{a..}~u \arrow[ld,equal] \\
    & \subs{b_l}{a..}~u
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Equational theories}

To formally relate reduction to biCCCs, we can define a notion of conversion
\(\conv{\Gamma}{t}{t'}[A]\) capturing equality of morphisms in a biCCC
as the least congruent equivalence relation containing
\(\beta\) and \(\eta\) laws, encoding the
universal property of each type former.
These are strictly stronger than
the commuting conversions of \cref{fig:stlc-red}; indeed we have
\[\conv
  {x : A + A, y : A + A, z : A}
  {\ite{x}{\ite{y}{z}{z}}{\ite{y}{z}{z}}}
  {\ite{y}{\ite{x}{z}{z}}{\ite{x}{z}{z}}}
  [A]
\]
by \(\eta\) for sums, but the two terms are not related by commuting conversions
alone.

But this is not an issue for us, as our interpolation proof only uses conversion
in covariant positions, so we show stronger theorems using reduction,
and can then weaken them to conversion, owing to the following.
\begin{lemma}[Subject reduction]
  If \(\typing{\Gamma}{t}[A]\) and \(\red{t}{u}\) then \(\conv{\Gamma}{t}{u}[A]\).
\end{lemma}
We actually go further and define a notion of \emph{theory},
which extends the specification of a language with
a set of equations that are added to its conversion.
This barely changes the proofs, but means that
our interpolation can be leveraged to prove general properties of
biCCCs by viewing \stlc as their internal language,
although we do not explore this here.

\section{Bidirectional typing, normal forms and the subformula property}

In logic, a key characteristic of derivations is the \emph{subformula property}, satisfied
when where every formula appearing in the derivation is a subformula of some formula
in the conclusion judgment ---either as a hypothesis or conclusion. This
does not hold of arbitrary derivations, but is satisfied by derivations which
are sufficiently normalised, \ie cut-free proofs \cite{Girard1989}.
In a derivation satisfying the subformula property, no formula is ever “invented”,
a powerful structural property with many nice consequences. In
particular, interpolation is usually much easier to prove for cut-free derivation
\cite{??}. One typically obtains interesting properties for all provable
statements by a combination of a cut elimination theorem, which says that every
proof is equal to a cut-free one, and an analysis of cut-free proofs, which can
exploit its more constrained structure, including the subformula property.

For a type theorist, this might seem a bit mysterious. Cut elimination, by
the Curry-Howard correspondence, is well-known to coincide with reduction of
terms viewed as proof witnesses. However, what does the subformula property
amount to? This is much less known, but it can be very helpfully linked to
\emph{bidirectional typing}.%
\footnote{We are not the first to make this connection: it appears
in Dunfield and Krishnaswami's survey on the topic~\cite[Sec. 8]{Dunfield2021},
which attribute it to Pfenning, although we were not able to find a reference
to that effect. The relation between normal forms and the subformula property is also
mentioned by Abel and Sattler~\cite{Abel2019}, although without drawing the
connection to bidirectional typing.}

In bidirectional typing,
which originates from type-checking algorithms \cite{Coquand1996,Pierce2000},
the typing judgment is decomposed into the two, mutually defined
\emph{inference} and \emph{checking} judgments. The operational intuition is
that in the former the type is an output,%
\footnote{\textit{I.e.} the question is ``What is the type of this term?''.}
while in the latter it is an input.%
\footnote{And the question is ``Does this term have this type?''.}
This attention to the flow of information is, in a sense, also what
the subformula property intends to capture: in both cases, the core property
is that type information always comes from somewhere and is never invented.
And in fact bidirectional typing can helpfully be leveraged to exploit
this subformula property, see \eg the strengthening proof in
Lennon-Bertrand~\cite{LennonBertrand2021}.

With this view it is perhaps unsurprising that bidirectional
type systems can be used to structurally characterise normal forms, since,
as we just explained, these correspond to cut-free proofs which have a good
subformula property. This is, however, a remark with powerful consequences:
because bidirectional type systems have a nice, inductive structure, they
give a good basis on which to build clean proof of theorems that rely on
the subformula property, such as interpolation. The remainder of this article
is dedicated to delivering that idea: the rest of this section sets up the
bidirectional characterisation of normal forms, while \cref{sec:interpolation}
derives the interpolation result.

\subsection{A bidirectional characterisation of normal forms}

Our bidirectional type system is given in \cref{fig:bidir}, and was heavily
inspired by the normal forms of Abel and Sattler~\cite{Abel2019}
---suitably modified to not enforce \(\eta\)-long forms. It can be interpreted in two ways.

In the bidirectional mindset, the rules can be read as
a typing algorithm for a fragment of \stlcp. Rules for the inference judgment
\(\infer{\Gamma}{t}{A}\) correspond to settings where it is feasible to infer
the type \(A\) from \(t\), by inferring that of a carefully chosen subterm
(in the case of a negative elimination \nameref{rule:app-b},\nameref{rule:proji-b})
or directly reading it from the
local (\nameref{rule:var-b}) or global (\nameref{rule:cst-b}) context.
Conversely, rules for the checking judgments are those where it is better to check
that a term has a given type. This encompasses constructor forms
(\nameref{rule:star-b}, \nameref{rule:pair-b}, \nameref{rule:lam-b}, \nameref{rule:inl-b},
\nameref{rule:inr-b}), where the checked type is decomposed into its parts
which are used to adequately check the subterms of the constructor;%
\footnote{An acute reader could wonder why we do not make \nameref{rule:star-b}
infer, as \(\star\) can only be of type \(\top\).
Although this could indeed be done, since \(\star\) can
only appear in a checking position because of typing constraints this would
make little difference, and it is more uniform to treat \(\star\)
as a nullary version of pairing.}
any inferring term, which we can also check provided the inferred type coincides
with the checked one (\nameref{rule:demote-b}); and more, interestingly, the
elimination for positive types (\nameref{rule:raise-b}, \nameref{rule:if-b}).
It is rather clear that \(\rai\) cannot be reasonably inferring: how are
we supposed to guess the type \(A\)? For \(\iteop\), we could imagine inferring
the type of both branches, comparing the two, and if they agree infer that type
for the whole pattern-matching. However, our version ends up being better behaved.

In this view, the bidirectional type system arises purely from the will to
type terms without any annotations. To do so, we can only rely on type information
which is already available, either in the context \(\Gamma\), or in the type \(A\)
in the case of checking. This means that all the type information we use
should appear in these in one way or another, which, to reiterate,
is exactly what the subformula property says!

The other approach, coming from normalisation proofs, views the \(\check{\Gamma}{t}{A}\)
judgment as characterising \emph{normal forms}, those which do not reduce further.
When inductively characterising normal forms, it is necessary to mutually define
\emph{neutral forms}, those which can be safely used as the main argument of
an elimination form without creating redexes. Usually, any elimination of a neutral
is again a neutral. But in presence of commuting conversions, this will not do,
as an eliminator on top of \(\rai\) or \(\iteop\) reduces. So in that view too,
it makes sense that these are classified as normal forms but not neutrals.

We can prove that this adequately captures well-typed terms that do not reduce,
\ie that our inductive characterisation indeed captures normal forms in the
more traditional sense. Here we express the theorems only for normal forms,
but they of course also hold for neutrals.

\begin{lemma}[Typability]
  If \(\check{\Gamma}{t}{A}\), then \(\typing{\Gamma}{t}[A]\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Normal forms do not reduce]
  If \(\check{\Gamma}{t}{A}\), then there is no \(t'\) such that
  \(\ored{t}{t'}\).
\end{lemma}

The above two lemmas together show that normal forms are well-typed terms
which do not reduce. The progress theorem below gives a strong
converse to that fact.
\begin{theorem}[Progress]
  If \(\typing{\Gamma}{t}[A]\), then either there is \(t'\) such that
  \(\ored{t}{t'}\), or \(\check{\Gamma}{t}{A}\).
\end{theorem}

\subsection[Normalisation for STLCp]{Normalisation for \stlcp}

In \cref{sec:interpolation}, we prove interpolation for normal forms only.
To know that it applies more generally, we need to know that every well-typed term
reduces to a normal form. That is, we
need a normalisation theorem. As usual for such theorems, we give a proof by
logical relations, heavily inspired by Abel and Sattler~\cite{Abel2019},
although compared to their normalisation-by-evaluation approach we are able to
obtain a result about reduction rather than conversion.
% As for all logical relation argument, the proof goes by defining a semantic
% notion of typing, which implies normalisation, but is strengthened to be stable
% under the semantic version of all typing rules.

\paragraph*{Semantic typing}

In a logical relation argument, the key definition is that of values and reducibility,
which go by induction on types. Values at negative types are defined as those
normal forms whose observations are reducible: a value at a pair
type is a normal form whose first and second projections are values, and a value
at function type takes any value input to a reducible output. This
works fine here. However, things are trickier at positive types. Usually, one
would say that a value at a positive type is given either by a constructor
or a neutral. This will not do here, because we must take into account elimination
of positive types, which are not neutrals. This brings us to introduce
the following inductive notion of covering.

\begin{definition}[Covering]
  A term \(t \in \Tm(\Delta,A)\) is \emph{covered} by the predicate
    \(F \in (\Gamma \in \Ctx) \to \Tm(\Gamma,A) \to \Prop\)
  if either:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \(F(t)\) holds;
    \item \(t = \rai n\) with \(\infer{\Gamma}{n}{\bot}\);
    \item \(t = \ite{n}{b_l}{b_r}\), with \(\infer{\Gamma}{n}{A + B}\),
      and \(b_l\), \(b_r\) are both covered (respectively in \(\Gamma.(x:A)\) and
      \(\Gamma.(x:B)\)).
  \end{itemize}
  We say that a term \(t \in \Tm(\Gamma,A)\) is “covered by neutrals” if it is covered
  by the predicate \(F(\Delta,u) \Coloneqq \infer{\Delta}{u}{A}\), and similarly
  for other predicates on terms.
\end{definition}
This captures terms which have the shape of a case tree,
with nodes decorated by eliminations of neutrals at positive types, and leafs
by terms satisfying the given predicate (in a suitably extended context). It
echoes that of the free cover monad in \textit{op.\ cit.}~\cite{Abel2019}.

The next definition of values and reducible terms goes by induction
on types.
\begin{definition}[Values]
  A \emph{raw value} at a type \(T\) is a term \(t \in \Tm(\Gamma,T)\)
  such that:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item if \(T\) is a base type or \(\bot\) and \(t\) is neutral;
    \item \(T\) is \(\top\) and \(t\) is either neutral or \(\star\);
    \item \(T\) is \(A + B\) and \(t\) is either neutral, \(\inl(a)\) with
      \(a\) value of type \(A\) in \(\Gamma\), or \(\inr(b)\) with \(b\)
      value of type \(B\) in \(\Gamma\).
  \end{itemize}

  A \emph{value} at type \(T\) is a term \(v \in \Tm(\Gamma,T)\) such that:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item if \(T\) is a positive type (base type, \(\bot\), \(A + B\)), \(v\) is
      covered by raw values;
    \item if \(T\) is \(\top\), \(v\) is a normal form;
    \item if \(T\) is \(A_1 \times A_2\), \(v\) is a normal form and \(\proj{i}{v}\)
      is reducible at \(A_i\);
    \item if \(T\) is \(A \to B\), \(v\) is a normal form and for any
      context \(\Delta\), renaming \(\typing{\Delta}{\rho}[\Gamma]\),
      and value \(u \in \Tm(\Delta,A)\), \(\subs{v}{\rho}~u\) is reducible.
  \end{itemize}
  
  Finally, a term \(t\) is \emph{reducible} if there exists a value \(v\) such
  that \(\lred{t}{v}\).
\end{definition}
We extend this definition to substitution pointwise, \ie a value substitution
is a substitution \(\sigma \in \Sub(\Delta,\Gamma)\) mapping each variable
\((x : A) \Gamma\) to a value \(\sigma(x)\) at \(A\).
We use this to define semantic typing.
\begin{definition}[Semantic typing]
  A term \(t\) is \emph{semantically well-typed}, written \(\semty{\Gamma}{t}[A]\),
  if for any value substitution \(\sigma \in \Sub(\Delta,\Gamma)\), \(\subs{t}{\sigma}\)
  is reducible at \(A\) in \(\Delta\).
\end{definition}

\paragraph*{Properties}

Before we can get on to proving the semantic counterparts to the typing rules in
\cref{fig:stlc}, we first need a handful of properties of covers, values and
reducible terms.

\begin{lemma}[Reification]
  Any value is a normal form.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Reflection]
  \label{lem:reflect}
  Any neutral is a value. 
\end{lemma}
These lemmas are named by analogy with the reify/reflect functions of normalisation by
evaluation, which respectively map neutrals into the semantic domain and
map semantic values back to normal forms. Proofs go by induction on the
types. For the first, the key ingredient is that a term covered by normal
forms is again a normal form. We need reification in reflection, to handle
reducibility at function types where to conclude that applying the neutral
to a value yields a neutral we need to turn that value into a normal form.

Our next goal is to show that a term covered by reducible terms is itself reducible.
At positive types, this is easy, thanks to the following, which can be read as
the multiplication/join of a covering monad.
\begin{lemma}
  A term covered by terms covered by \(F\) is covered by \(F\).
\end{lemma}

At negative types, however, things are not that simple. Consider the function type:
we assume that at each leaf of the cover we have terms which, when applied to
a value, yield a reducible result; and need to deduce that the whole covered term
is itself reducible, that is, yields reducible result when applied to a value.
The first ingredient is the following lemma, which pushes application
to the leaves of a cover by virtue of commuting conversions.

\begin{lemma}
  \label{lem:push-app}
  If \(f \in \Tm(\Gamma,A\to B)\) is covered by \(F\) and \(u \in \Tm(\Gamma,A)\),
  then \(\red{f~u}{t}\) for some \(t\) which is covered by the predicate
  \[\Delta,t' \mapsto \exists (\rho : \Ren(\Delta,\Gamma)) (f' : \Tm(\Delta,A \to B)).
  (t' = f'~\subs{u}{\rho}) \wedge (F~f')\]
\end{lemma}

We omit similar lemmas for the other destructors: projections, \(\rai\)
and \(\iteop\).
Because pushing the argument to the leaves introduces extra variables
in the context, we must also know that values are preserved by context extension.
The need for this preservation by renaming is what motivates the Kripke-style
quantification over renamings in the definition of values at function
types, without which it would not hold.
For the category theorist, this should be
unsurprising: we wish our normal forms to form a presheaf over
renamings, and the definition of the semantic function type thus mirrors that
of presheaf exponentials.

\begin{lemma}
  If \(v\) is a value of type \(A\) in \(\Gamma\) and \(\rho \in \Ren(\Delta,\Gamma)\),
  then \(\subs{v}{\rho}\) is a value.
\end{lemma}

Putting these together, we obtain:

\begin{lemma}[Reducible cover]
  \label{lem:red-cover}
  A term covered by reducible terms is reducible.
\end{lemma}

With this key lemma in hand, the rest of the semantic typing proofs is
history, and we let the reader consult the \Rocq development for details.
An interesting point to note, though, is that the analogue of \cref{lem:push-app}
for \(\rai\) and \(\iteop\) is not used to prove \cref{lem:red-cover}, but 
instead appear when showing the semantic typing rule for
the eliminators: owing to the polarity of the types, the work for
negative types is in handling the constructors ---where we need \cref{lem:red-cover}---,
while destructors are the hard part for positive types.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental lemma]
  If \(\typing{\Gamma}{t}[A]\) then \(\semty{\Gamma}{t}[A]\).
\end{theorem}

From \cref{lem:reflect} we get that variables are values at all types, and
thus that the identity substitution is a value substitution. Hence, we conclude

\begin{corollary}[Normalisation]
  Any well-typed term reduces to a normal form.
\end{corollary}

\section{Interpolation}
\label{sec:interpolation}

With our characterisation of normal forms in hand, we can move to the
main interpolation result. Following \Cubric~\cite{Cubric1994}, we give a
polarised version in the fashion of Lyndon interpolation \cite{Lyndon1959interpolation} rather than the simpler Craig interpolation, and
thus start with a polarised notion of atoms.

\begin{definition}[Atoms]
  Let \(A \in \Ty_{\mathcal{B}}\). We define \(A^+ \subseteq \mathcal{B}\)
    (resp.\ \(A^- \subseteq \mathcal{B}\)), the \emph{positive}
    (resp.\ \emph{negative}) \emph{atoms} of \(A\) by induction, with $\overline{p}=+$ (resp. $-$) \emph{iff} $p=-$ (resp. $+$):\\[-1.5em]
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.15\textwidth}
  \begin{align*}
    b^{+} & \coloneq \{b\} \\
    b^{-} & \coloneq \emptyset
  \end{align*}
  \end{minipage}
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.15\textwidth}
  \begin{align*}
    \top^p & \coloneq \emptyset \\
    \bot^p & \coloneq \emptyset \\
  \end{align*}
  \end{minipage}
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.25\textwidth}
  \begin{align*}
    (A + B)^{p} & \coloneq A^p \cup B^p \\
    (A \times B)^{p} & \coloneq A^p \cup B^p \\
  \end{align*}
  \end{minipage}
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.2\textwidth}
  \begin{align*}
    (A \to B)^{p} & \coloneq A^{\overline{p}} \cup B^p \\
  \end{align*}
  \end{minipage}\\
This is lifted pointwise to contexts as
  \((\Gamma.(x : A))^p \coloneq \Gamma^p \cup A^p\).
\end{definition}

Although stating the final theorem does not need it, in the proof by induction
it is necessary to keep track of which variables in the context are “coming from”
the conclusion, and thus we need to maintain a context partition in the following sense.

\begin{definition}[Context partition]
  \emph{Context partition} \(\Gamma = \part{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{2}}\)
  is the relation defined by
  \begin{mathpar}
    \inferdef{ }{\emp = \part{\emp}{\emp}} \and
    \inferdef{\Gamma = \part{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{2}}}{
      \Gamma. (x : A) = \part{\Gamma_{1}.(x : A)}{\Gamma_{2}}} \and
    \inferdef{\Gamma = \part{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{2}}}{
      \Gamma. (x : A) = \part{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{2}. (x : A)}}
  \end{mathpar}
\end{definition}

In a named setting this is a mere relation: as long as we enforce that variable
names in contexts are unique, they tell us how the partitioning is done.
In the formalisation, where we use a nameless syntax, context partition becomes
structure: there are two proofs of \(A.A = \part{A}{A}\), respectively corresponding,
in a named setting, to \((x:A).(y:A) = \part{(x:A)}{(y:A)}\) and
\((x:A).(y:A) = \part{(y:A)}{(x:A)}\). We spare the reader the substitution
calculus arising from this, \eg the fact that if \(\Gamma = \part{\Gamma_1}{\Gamma_2}\)
then there is a renaming \(\typing{\Gamma}{\rho}[\scol{\Gamma_1}]\).

\subsection{Constant-free}

Our first version of interpolation works for a constant-free language: for
the remainder of this section we fix a language \(\lang = (\base,\emptyset,\_)\).

\begin{theorem}[Interpolation, constant-free]
  Assume \(\typing[\lang]{\Gamma}{t}[T]\), then there exists a type \(M\) and terms
  \(\typing{\Gamma}{l}[M]\) and \(\typing{(x:M)}{r}[T]\) such that
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \(M\) uses only the atoms common to \(\Gamma\) and \(T\):
    for any \(p \in \{+,-\}\) \(M^p \subseteq \Gamma^p \cap T^p\);
  \item \(t\) is the composition of \(r\) and \(l\): \(\conv{\Gamma}{t}{\subs{r}{l..}}[T]\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

Since by normalisation every term is equal to a normal form, it is enough
to show the following theorem for normal forms,
and specialize it to the trivial partition \(\Gamma = \part{\Gamma}{\emp}\).

\begin{theorem}[Interpolation, inductive statement]
  Assume a partitioned context \(\Gamma = \part{\Gamma_{s}}{\Gamma_{t}}\).
  %
  Given any normal form \(\ccol{t} \in \Nf(\Gamma,\tcol{T})\),
  there is a type \(\mcol{M}\) and terms
  \(\scol{l} \in \Tm(\scol{\Gamma_{s}},\mcol{M})\) and
  \(\tcol{r} \in \Tm(\tcol{\Gamma_{t}}.\mcol{(x : M)}, \tcol{T})\)
  such that
  \begin{itemize}
    \item for all \(p\),
    \(\mcol{M}^p \subseteq \scol{\Gamma_{s}}^p \cap (\tcol{\Gamma_{t}}^{-p} \cup \tcol{T}^p)\);
    \item and \(\conv{\Gamma}{\subs{\tcol{r}}{\scol{l}..}}{\ccol{t}}[\tcol{T}]\). 
  \end{itemize}
  %
  Moreover, given any neutral term \(\icol{t} \in \Ne(\Gamma,T)\), either
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item “\(\scol{T}\) is a source type”, \ie \(\scol{T}^p \subseteq \scol{\Gamma_{s}}^p\),
      and we have a type \(\mcol{M}\) and terms \(\tcol{l} \in \Tm(\tcol{\Gamma_{t}},\mcol{M})\) and
  \(\scol{r} \in \Tm(\scol{\Gamma_{s}}.\mcol{(x : M)}, \scol{T})\) such that
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\mcol{M}^p \subseteq \scol{\Gamma_{s}}^{-p} \cap \tcol{\Gamma_{t}}^p\),
        \item and \(\conv{\Gamma}{\subs{\scol{r}}{\tcol{l}..}}{\icol{t}}[\scol{T}]\);
      \end{itemize}
    \item or “\(\tcol{T}\) is a target type”, \ie for all \(p\),
      \(\tcol{T}^p \subseteq \tcol{\Gamma_{t}}^p\), and we have a type \(\mcol{M}\)
      and terms \(\scol{l} \in \Tm(\scol{\Gamma_{s}},\mcol{M})\) and
      \(\tcol{r} \in \Tm(\tcol{\Gamma_{t}}.\mcol{(x : M)}, \tcol{T})\) such that
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \(\mcol{M}^p \subseteq \scol{\Gamma_{s}}^p \cap \tcol{\Gamma_{t}}^{-p}\),
        \item \(\conv{\Gamma}{\subs{\tcol{r}}{\scol{l}..}}{\icol{t}}[\scol{T}]\)
      \end{itemize}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The first part of the statement, for normal forms, is a generalisation of
the interpolation judgment using the partition to take into account that some
type information initially in the target type can be moved in the context,
\eg in the case of a \(\lambda\)-abstraction. This is more or less the content
of the main lemma in the original proof \cite[Lemma 3.7]{Cubric1994}.
Its counterpart for neutrals, however, was not at all explicit in the original
proof, which did not use neutrals altogether. Instead, the proof went by strong
induction on the size of terms, replacing subterms by variables, which
we replace by a much more direct reasoning by mutual induction.
An interesting aspect to note is that, in case 1.\ for neutrals,
interpolation is reversed, in that the roles of the source and target context
are exchanged in the typing of the interpolants compared to the statement for normal forms.

\begin{proof}
As expected, the proof goes by induction on the definition of \(\Ne/\Nf\).\\
  %
The \(\Nf\) cases are rather direct.\\
  %
\nameref{rule:star-b}: \(\tcol{T}\) is \(\top\), so we can take
  \(\mcol{M} = \top\), and \(\scol{l} = \tcol{r} = \star\).\\
  %
  % \nameref{rule:pair-b}: \(\tcol{T}\) is \(\tcol{T_1} \times \tcol{T_2}\), \(\ccol{t}\)
  % is \(\ccol{(t_1,t_2)}\). By induction,
  % we obtain \(\scol{l_i} \in \Tm(\scol{\Gamma_{s}},\mcol{M_i})\)
  % and \(\scol{r_i} \in \Tm(\tcol{\Gamma_{t}}.\mcol{(x : M_i)},\tcol{T_i})\).
  % We can then take \(\mcol{M} \coloneq \mcol{M_1 \times M_2}\) and
  % \[\begin{array}{rlll}
  %   \scol{\Gamma_{s}} \vdash & \scol{l} \coloneq &
  %     \scol{(l_1,l_2)} & \ty \mcol{M_1 \times M_2} \\
  %   \tcol{\Gamma_{t}}.\mcol{(x : M_1 \times M_2)} \vdash &
  %     \tcol{r} \coloneq & \tcol{(\subs{r_1}{\proj{1}{y}..},\subs{r_2}{\proj{2}{y}..})} &
  %     \ty \tcol{T_1 \times T_2}
  % \end{array}\]
  %
  \nameref{rule:lam-b}: We extend the \emph{target} context \(\tcol{\Gamma_{t}}\)
  with the extra variable (this is why we need the formulation with a context partition!)
  and by induction hypothesis, we obtain
  \(\scol{l'} \in \Tm(\scol{\Gamma_{s}},\mcol{M})\) and
  \(\tcol{r'} \in \Tm(\tcol{\Gamma_{t}}.\tcol{(x : A).\mcol{(z : M)}})\),
  and we can take
  \[\begin{array}{rlll}
  \scol{\Gamma_{s}} \vdash & \scol{l} \coloneq & \scol{l'} & \ty \mcol{M} \\
  \tcol{\Gamma_{t}}.\mcol{(z : M)} \vdash & \tcol{r} \coloneq &
    \tcol{\l x.r'} &
    \ty \tcol{A \to B}
  \end{array}\]
  %
  \nameref{rule:pair-b}, \nameref{rule:inl-b}, \nameref{rule:inr-b} are similar:
  we post-compose \(\tcol{r}\) as obtained by recursive interpolation
  by the relevant constructor, except we do not need to think about context extension.

  The \(\Ne\) cases are more interesting. For each rule, we have two subcases,
  depending on whether the induction hypothesis for the main sub-neutral is
  in case 1 or 2 above, \ie on whether the main variable of the neutral
  is in the source context or target context.\\
  %
  \nameref{rule:var-b}/source: We have \((x \ty \scol{A}) \in \scol{\Gamma_s}\).
  Then we take \(\mcol{M} \coloneq \top\)
  \[\begin{array}{rlll}

\subsection{Constants and equations}

We can generalise the above to arbitrary languages \(\lang = (\base,\const,\tyof)\)
with arbitrary equations.
As explained in \cref{sec:red-eq}, handling the extra equations is essentially
trivial. As for constants, we have to be a bit careful that
the interpolant might also use atoms present in the constants. The
final theorem is the following:

\begin{theorem}[Interpolation, with constants]
  Assume \(\typing[\lang]{\Gamma}{t}[T]\), and a partition
  \(\const = \const_{s} \cup \const_{t}\) of constants.
  Define \(\const_{i}^p \coloneq \cup_{c \in \const_{i}} (\tyof c)^p\)
    the atoms of the sets of constants, and
    \(\lang_{i} \coloneq (\base,\const_{i},\tyof_{i})\)
    where \(\tyof_{i}\) is the obvious restriction.
  Then there exists a type \(M\) and terms
  \(\typing[\lang_{l}]{\Gamma}{l}[M]\) and \(\typing[\lang_{r}]{(x:M)}{r}[T]\) such that
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \(M\) uses only the atoms common to \(\Gamma\) and \(\const_{s}\)
    on one side, and \(T\) and \(\const_{t}\) on the other:
    for any \(p \in \{+,-\}\)
    \(M^p \subseteq (\Gamma^p \cup \const_{s}^p) \cap (T^p \cup \const_{t}^p)\);
  \item \(t\) is the composition of \(r\) and \(l\): \(\conv{\Gamma}{t}{\subs{r}{l..}}[T]\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Future work}

In terms of future work, we envision three particularly interesting directions for future work, each corresponding to strengthening the result of the present paper in one orthogonal direction: (i) interpolating non-normal terms, (ii) proof-relevant uniform interpolation and (iii) extending \v Cubri\' c' results from simple types to dependent types.

\paragraph*{Extensions with cuts}
The first, evident, direction for future work consist in solving the first limitation of \v Cubri\' c' interpolation which only applies to normal forms. From the perspective of the computational interpretation of the result, this is indeed a drastic limitation: every computation can be factored through an interface type in the common type language of the input and output data types, but only under the assumption that one first normalise the term to normal form. 
What if the term is not in normal form? 
Considering that interpolation in fact requires a much weaker condition than the subformula property, one can hope to easily captures terms with cuts satisfying some assumptions. 

First results in this direction have been done by Fiorillo {\it et al}~\cite{fiorillo26} where they extend Saurin's results to sequent proofs with cut satisfying some vocabulary constraints. 
We believe that similar results can be transported to \stlcp and see at least two paths to reach such a result:
\begin{itemize}
\item In their work, Fiorillo {\it et al}~\cite{fiorillo26} identity quite general condition on cut formulas so that sequent calculus proof-relevant interpolation can be carried out in the presence of cut inferences and they moreover show that proof-relevant interpolation can be achieved for MELL proof-nets with cuts (under similar assumptions as above). Since one can encode the simply typed lambda-calculus in the intuitionistic fragment of MELL using linear embeddings of intuitionistic logic, one can hope to establish a similar result for $\lambda$-terms with redexes via those translations to proof-nets. There are still some difficulties to achieve such a result in this direction amounting to the need to design a faithful embedding of the whole of \stlcp in IMELL that satisfies a good simulation result. 
\item Another direction is to follow the methodology considered in the present paper and to design a bidirectional type system that does not characterize only normal forms but also those terms having redexes that satisfy the assumptions of the interpolation theorem with cuts. This will require having a more relaxed systems allowing some annotations of terms as long as those annotations guarantee the vocabulary constraints needed for interpolation.   
\end{itemize}


\paragraph*{Uniform interpolation} (for the \stlc)
\cite{Feree2024}

A very natural and exciting question is the following: can the proof-relevant interpolation result presented above be "uniformised"? 
While the current result ensures that given types $A$ and $B$ and a normal term $x: A \vdash t:B$, there exists a type $I$ satisfying the vocabulary conditions and two terms $u,v$ \emph{depending on} $A,B$ and $t$, such that $ \red{(\lambda y. u)v}{t}$, a uniform proof relevant interpolation could be stated in two ways:
\begin{description}
\item[uniform] For every type $A$, for every $L \subseteq \base$ there exists $I$ such that $voc(I)\subseteq L$ such that for any $B$ satisfying $voc(A)\cap voc(B) \subseteq L$, and every $x:A \vdash t: B$, there are terms $u,v$ such that $y: I\vdash u: B$ and $x: A\vdash v: I$ and $ \red{(\lambda y. u)v}{t}$.
\item[strongly uniform] For every type $A$, for every $L \subseteq \base$ there exists $I$ such that $voc(I)\subseteq L$ and $x: A \vdash v: I$  such that for any $B$ satisfying $voc(A)\cap voc(B) \subseteq L$, and every $x:A \vdash t: B$, there are terms $u$ such that $y: I\vdash u: B$ and $ \red{(\lambda y. u)v}{t}$.
\end{description}
